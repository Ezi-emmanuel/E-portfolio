    Module: Launch into Computing

    Cybersecurity Threat Assessment and Mitigation Plan

    by Eziuche Emmanuel

    Scenario and Threat Assessment

    This report examines a mid-sized healthcare enterprise currently facing targeted ransomware attacks. The organization relies on centralized relational databases and distributed cloud microservices to manage confidential patient telemetry and payroll records. Migrating sensitive healthcare data to distributed, expanding cloud environments fundamentally alters the risk profile, introducing significant vulnerabilities that attackers frequently exploit (Cilluffo, 2022).

    The primary threat vector is ransomware, malicious software designed to infiltrate networks, encrypt operational databases, and extort payment. The enterprise is particularly exposed due to unpatched endpoint operating systems across administrative workstations. These vulnerabilities allow ransomware payloads to execute remote code and move laterally across the network. Furthermore, the organization is hindered by human-centric psychological vulnerabilities. Spear-phishing campaigns consistently deceive personnel, providing the initial entry point for attackers. Effective threat modeling must account for these vulnerabilities in user interface design, as poor interactions often facilitate credential theft (Akinsola and Ebenezer, 2021). Once valid credentials are compromised, adversaries gain unrestricted access to the infrastructure, effectively freezing clinical pipelines, locking patient databases, and halting core medical operations.

    Mitigation Strategies and Justifications

    To neutralize these threats, the enterprise must implement a rigorous defense-in-depth strategy. First, Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) must be mandated across all system entry points. MFA mitigates credential harvesting risks by requiring an independent validation token—such as a biometric or hardware-based authenticator—before access is granted. This control prevents attackers from utilizing stolen passwords to deploy payloads.

    Second, the organization must enforce the principle of least privilege within its Identity and Access Management (IAM) framework. By confining database access to role-specific requirements, the enterprise reduces its internal attack surface. If an administrative account is compromised, least privilege effectively restricts lateral movement, preventing widespread network encryption. Third, robust cryptographic encryption is required for data protection. The enterprise must apply the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES-256) for all data at-rest within storage databases. Concurrently, Transport Layer Security (TLS 1.3) must be mandated for all active network traffic and API communications. Encryption ensures that even if actors exfiltrate data, the intercepted information remains useless for secondary extortion schemes (Chang, 2022).

    Ethical, Legal, and Compliance Considerations

    Implementing these mitigations involves significant legal and ethical responsibilities. Legally, the enterprise must ensure strict adherence to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Since the databases house sensitive medical records, any unauthorized data exposure or availability loss constitutes a direct violation of Article 5 data minimization and security principles. Compliance mandates prompt incident reporting and robust protection measures to avoid substantial regulatory penalties. Furthermore, the organization should align its security posture with the ISO/IEC 27001 framework, establishing a comprehensive Information Security Management System (ISMS) to systematically manage ongoing risks.

    Ethically, cybersecurity professionals hold a fundamental duty to protect user privacy and maintain system transparency. Safeguarding digital assets transcends basic regulatory checklists; it is a moral imperative to ensure that patient care remains available during crises. As Walton (2024) emphasizes, the design of computing systems must remain responsive to the needs of the diverse populations they serve. Therefore, designing inclusive, resilient computing systems guarantees that organizational continuity is maintained without unnecessarily compromising individual privacy rights. Treating security as a social responsibility strengthens institutional legitimacy, thereby better protecting the most vulnerable users across its network.
