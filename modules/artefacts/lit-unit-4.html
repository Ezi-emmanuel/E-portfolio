    Comparative Analysis of Software Development Methodologies

    by Eziuche Emmanuel

    The shift from traditional linear software development models toward adaptive, collaborative approaches has positioned Agile and DevOps as two of the most influential methodologies in contemporary software engineering. Although frequently implemented together, they serve distinct purposes and exhibit unique strengths and limitations that influence their suitability for different project contexts.

    1. Flexibility and Adaptability

    Agile is designed around the expectation that requirements will evolve throughout the development lifecycle. Through iterative sprints and continuous stakeholder engagement, Agile enables rapid adaptation and refinement of product features (Stephens, 2022). Its primary strength lies in its responsiveness to change, making it ideal for projects with uncertain or emerging requirements. However, Agile's reliance on frequent communication and iterative cycles can become challenging in highly regulated environments or in teams lacking strong collaboration structures.

    DevOps extends adaptability beyond development by integrating operations, automation, and deployment processes. Practices such as Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment (CI/CD) and Infrastructure as Code (IaC) enable rapid, reliable delivery of software changes (Humble and Farley, 2010). DevOps' strength lies in its ability to reduce deployment risk and accelerate delivery pipelines. Its limitation is the substantial initial investment required in tooling, automation, and cultural transformation, which may be disproportionate for very small or short-term projects.

    2. Suitability for Project Scale

    Agile scales effectively from small teams to large enterprises when supported by frameworks such as Scrum or SAFe (Dingsøyr et al., 2012). Its lightweight documentation and iterative cycles support rapid prototyping, but scaling Agile requires disciplined coordination and can introduce communication overhead.

    DevOps is particularly advantageous in medium- to large-scale systems where automation significantly reduces operational bottlenecks. The methodology enhances reliability, consistency, and deployment speed—critical factors in complex, distributed systems (Greenaway, 2023). However, for small projects with limited infrastructure needs, the cost and complexity of implementing full DevOps pipelines may outweigh the benefits.

    3. Risk Management and Quality Assurance

    Agile mitigates risk by delivering functional increments early, enabling teams to validate assumptions and reduce the likelihood of late-stage failures (Stephens, 2022). Its limitation is that rapid iteration may overlook long-term architectural considerations if not carefully managed.

    DevOps strengthens quality assurance through automated testing, monitoring, and deployment. Automated pipelines support a fail-fast culture, reducing human error and ensuring consistent environments across development and production (Bass, Weber and Zhu, 2015). The limitation lies in the dependency on robust automation infrastructure, which requires ongoing maintenance and specialised expertise.

    4. Recommendations for Clinical AI Scenarios

    • Clinical AI Prototyping: Agile is the most suitable methodology for early-stage clinical AI tools. Rapid iteration and clinician-centred feedback loops ensure alignment with clinical workflows and reduce the risk of developing misaligned or unsafe features.
    • Enterprise Hospital Management Systems: DevOps is recommended for large-scale hospital information systems requiring reliability, security, and continuous regulatory updates. Automated deployment pipelines and operational consistency are essential in environments where downtime directly impacts patient care.

    In conclusion, Agile and DevOps are complementary. Agile cultivates customer-focused adaptation, while DevOps provides the technical foundation for reliable, high-speed delivery. A mature software engineering approach often requires the integration of both to balance development agility with operational stability.

    AI-Driven Diagnostics in Modern Healthcare

    Initial post

    by Eziuche Emmanuel

    Introduction

    The evolution of computing has transitioned from the calculating machines described by O’Regan (2021) to autonomous systems capable of complex pattern recognition. Today, Artificial Intelligence (AI) serves as a transformative force in the healthcare industry, shifting the focus from reactive treatment to predictive analytics.

    Impact in healthcare, critical analysis and ethical concerns

    AI’s primary impact lies in its ability to manage big data through advanced algorithms. This is a concept Havill (2021) identifies as the core of the discipline. In clinical settings, AI optimizes diagnostic accuracy by identifying patterns in medical imaging that are often imperceptible to the human eye. This represents a significant layer of abstraction, where raw pixel data is transformed into actionable clinical insights (Najjar, 2023).

    A notable example occurred in late 2023 with the widespread adoption of AI-enabled mammography screening. A major German clinical trial involving over 460,000 scans demonstrated that AI-assisted radiologists detected 17.6% more cancers compared to human-only interpretation, without increasing false positives (Alowais et al., 2023). This highlights how computing disciplines converge to alleviate the global shortage of specialist clinicians.

    However, as Walton (2024) notes, technology is not culturally neutral. AI adoption introduces critical ethical challenges:

    • Algorithmic Bias: If training datasets lack diversity, AI models may underperform on minority populations, exacerbating existing healthcare disparities (Biswas, 2024).
    • The Black Box Problem: The lack of “interpretability” in deep learning makes it difficult for clinicians to justify AI-led decisions, raising significant liability concerns.
    Conclusion

    While AI offers unprecedented efficiency, its successful integration requires a socio-technical approach that balances computational power with ethical oversight and cultural responsiveness.

    Peer response 1

    Re: Initial Post — Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Banking: Consumer Lending

    Great points, Bernard and Olha. I think Olha hit on a really practical issue: even if a bank provides a “technical explanation” like a SHAP chart, it doesn’t mean much to a person who just had their loan denied.

    I’d like to extend this by looking at a risk called “fairwashing.” Essentially, this is when a model is tweaked to look perfectly fair on a transparency report, while the actual logic underneath is still using biased data to make decisions (Slack et al., 2020). It’s a bit like “gaming the system” to pass a test without actually being fair.

    This brings us back to the idea that technology isn’t neutral rather, it’s a “cultural mirror” (Walton, 2024). If we only try to fix the bias after the AI is built, we are just putting a band-aid on the problem. Following Havill’s (2021) view that computing is really about solving complex, real-world problems, I believe we need to treat fairness just like we treat cybersecurity. It shouldn’t be an afterthought or a technical report at the end; it has to be part of the design from the very first line of code. For AI in banking to be truly inclusive, the “explanation” needs to be understandable to the borrower, not just the data scientist.

    Peer response 2

    Re: Impacts of Cybersecurity on the Banking Industry

    This is a great breakdown of why banks have had to treat cybersecurity as a top-tier priority. It is fascinating to see how massive breaches, like the one at JPMorgan, act as a global wake-up call for the entire industry.

    I’d like to build on your points by looking at what is often called the “AI arms race”. While you mentioned that banks use AI to spot weird transactions, we have to remember that attackers are using those same tools to automate their strikes. As Havill (2021) explains, computing is basically about using logical steps to solve problems. For a hacker, that problem is finding a tiny hole in a bank’s defenses before the bank’s security team can patch it. This makes security a constant, high-speed battle of logic.

    I also think the human factor is just as important as the code. Walton (2024) reminds us that technology is always tied to the people using it. You can have the most expensive firewall in the world, but it won’t help if a tired employee clicks on a clever phishing link. This is why cybersecurity needs to be a cultural shift where everyone (not just the IT team) sees themselves as part of the defense. As Dasgupta (2022) suggests, the best security comes from balancing smart AI tools with a workforce that is trained to spot human-targeted tricks.

    Summary post

    A response to my initial post and further findings has really challenged my initial perspective on AI diagnostics. While it celebrated the 17.6% increase in cancer detection rates (Alowais et al., 2023), the feedback from Bernard was a real “eye-opener.” He pointed out that more detection doesn’t always mean better healthcare—sometimes it just leads to overdiagnosis, where we find things that might never have caused a problem, leading to unnecessary stress and treatment (Welch and Black, 2010).

    This critique helped me see a bigger picture. Initially, I viewed AI through the lens of abstraction and how it simplifies complex pixels into a “yes/no” diagnosis (Najjar, 2023). However, I now realize that computing is truly “interdisciplinary” in the sense Havill (2021) describes. It’s not just a math problem to solve; it’s a medical and ethical one. If we focus only on high detection numbers, we might miss the actual goal: better patient outcomes.

    I also found the points about algorithmic bias much more significant after our chat. Bernard noted that the “trade-offs” between missing a diagnosis and a false alarm aren’t the same for everyone. If an AI is less accurate for certain groups because of its training data, those patients bear a higher “risk burden” (Walton, 2024). This has shifted my thinking from seeing AI as a standalone tool to seeing it as part of a human-in-the-loop system.

    Ultimately, my growth in this unit has been moving from a “tech-first” view to a “human-first” view. AI is a powerful assistant, but as our discussion showed, it needs careful oversight to ensure it solves real-world problems without creating new ones.
